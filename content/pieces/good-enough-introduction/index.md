At ten to five on the morning of 3 September 1967, the traffic still moving on Sweden's roads came to a halt. Drivers crossed to the other side of the carriageway and waited. At five o'clock they set off again, on the right. The country had driven on the left until that moment, and it never did so again.

The odd thing about the change is how much it took. Driving on the left had been the sensible choice for every driver in Sweden, for the plain reason that everyone else did it. A driver who moved to the right alone would have been in mortal danger, and so would anyone coming the other way. When the question was put to voters in 1955, about eighty-three per cent of them chose to keep things as they were. Yet the neighbouring countries drove on the right, and most Swedish cars had the steering wheel on the left, which in left-hand traffic put the driver beside the verge and gave a poor view of the road ahead when overtaking. Parliament decided on the change in 1963 against the result of that vote. A commission spent the following years preparing it. New road signs went up under covers, junctions, signals and buses were altered, and the whole country agreed on a single minute.

Mathematicians have a name for the position Sweden was in. An arrangement is a *local minimum* when every change that can be made from it looks worse, or no better, even though a better arrangement exists further away. For an individual Swedish driver, every step from the left-hand side was a step into oncoming traffic. Whether the right-hand side was better for the country as a whole was a real question, and most of those who voted in 1955 thought it was not. What the idea explains is something else: why the move, if it was to happen at all, could not happen one driver at a time, and why it needed what it got.

This book is about that idea and what it can do outside mathematics. People often borrow a scientific term to explain why things go wrong. Entropy increases, they say, so desks grow untidy, organisations decay and plans fall apart. Used in that loose way, entropy explains decline and suggests nothing to do about it. A local minimum explains something more common and more useful: why sensible people, acting reasonably, stay with an arrangement when a better one is available. It does so without assuming that they are foolish, lazy or afraid of change, and it tells you what to ask next.

The questions are few. Is there a better arrangement, and better by whose measure? Why does every step from here look worse? What kind of barrier is it: something not yet known, a cost that cannot be paid now, a move that only pays if others make it too, earlier commitments that hold the present arrangement in place, or a measure that rewards the wrong thing? And what would make the move possible, and is it worth making? The rest of the book is an attempt to answer them for real cases, carefully enough that the answers can guide a decision.

The first part of the book looks at why arrangements persist. In a laboratory, observers counting passes in a basketball video fail to see a woman in a gorilla costume walk through the game. In a novel, a city guard captain cannot afford the good boots that would cost him less over ten years than the cheap ones he keeps buying. Two invented prisoners each serve five years because neither can trust the other to stay silent. In 1892 a railway company converts the last of its track to a gauge narrower than the one its own engineer had chosen, because the rest of Britain ran on it. A colonial government in Hanoi pays a bounty for rat tails and finds people breeding rats to collect it. Seoul removes the roads built over a stream in its centre, Vienna's doctors argue over handwashing, Kodak builds a digital camera and then keeps selling film, and a family's purchase of a house in Levittown, Pennsylvania, brings a crowd to their door.

The second part asks what makes a move possible. Engineers on the ground work out how to fit square canisters into round sockets so that three astronauts can breathe on the way back from the Moon. A bus boycott in Montgomery is sustained for a year by leaflets, meetings and carpools. A beginner's running programme starts with a single minute of jogging, a team at Lockheed has a prototype jet ready in 143 days, and villagers in Törbel, in the Swiss Alps, share their grazing and other common resources under an agreement first written down in 1483. Governments agree to stop making the chemicals that thin the ozone layer, and homeowners in Portland buy solar panels together. The third part asks when a move is not worth making. Belgium takes 541 days to form a government while its institutions go on working. Children in Beijing chase sparrows from the sky on official orders, a steel town in Ohio loses its works, and in rural Bangladesh a health worker's visit leaves a line marked on a family's cooking pot.

The idea has limits, and the book states them plainly where they matter. *Local minimum* has an exact meaning in mathematics, and human situations seldom fit it exactly: people want different things, conditions change, and nobody can list every alternative. Often the first question, whether the other arrangement is better at all, is the one in dispute, and an arrangement that looks stuck from outside may be the one its members would choose again. Chapter 3 and Chapter 14 take those limits seriously. The book's title comes from the other side of the same idea. Sometimes the place you are in is good enough, and the most useful thing the questions can do is show that a move is not worth its cost.

## How the book is arranged

The book is written for readers who make or influence decisions in a household, a workplace, a community or a public institution. It assumes no previous study of mathematics, economics, physics or programming. Technical terms are explained when they first appear, and optional appendices give the formal detail for readers who want it, together with a glossary, notes on disputed stories and a short guide to further reading.

Part I, Chapters 1 to 14, examines why arrangements persist, through the barriers named above and through cases in transport, healthcare, business and the places where people live. Part II, Chapters 15 to 23, examines what makes a change possible, at every scale from a single person to the world. Part III, Chapters 24 to 27, examines when a change is justified, when it should be resisted and who should decide.

The cases are documented ones, and the stories are told as the sources allow. Numbered citations in the text lead to notes grouped by chapter at the end of the book. The notes distinguish what was observed from what someone recalled, what a model shows from what happened, and an accepted account from a disputed one. Invented examples are marked as invented, and the one novel quoted is there because it isolates a difficulty clearly. The story of Sweden's change of side, with its sources, is told in Chapter 21.
