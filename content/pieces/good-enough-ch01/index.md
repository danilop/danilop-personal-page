During Stockholm’s congestion-charge trial, bus drivers reportedly had to slow down to avoid reaching their stops early. There was less traffic than their timetables allowed for. A British parliamentary delegation heard the account on a visit in July 2007, when it examined both the charging scheme and the contracts under which local buses operated. Those contracts required operators to follow an agreed schedule, which a faster journey gave nobody permission to change. [^good-enough-ch01-1]

The inconvenience was a consequence of an improvement. Passengers still needed buses to arrive when expected, including passengers who had not yet reached the stop. A timetable designed for congested streets could not make full use of clearer streets without being revised. The drivers’ response made sense within their instructions, even though the conditions that had helped determine those instructions had changed.

The congestion tax began on 3 January 2006, following an expansion of public transport. It applied to vehicles crossing a boundary around the inner city during charging hours and cost more at the busiest times. The charge was temporary: the trial would end in July, and residents would later vote on whether it should continue. Traffic crossing the boundary fell by roughly a fifth. [^good-enough-ch01-1][^good-enough-ch01-2]

This was a change in the circumstances of thousands of ordinary decisions. A journey that had previously involved fuel, parking and time now carried another cost. Travellers responded in several ways. About a quarter of the commuting trips made by car across the boundary disappeared, and nearly all of those commuters switched to public transport. Discretionary car trips fell by a similar proportion. For those journeys the main responses appear to have been choosing a different destination or travelling less often. When the charge was removed after the trial, traffic increased again, though it remained below its previous level. [^good-enough-ch01-2]

The transport system had produced one set of journeys under the previous rules and another under the trial. Neither result required everyone to agree about congestion charging, to make the same adjustment, or to become a different sort of person. A change in the available alternatives and their costs was enough to change many decisions.

The expansion of public transport that preceded the charge required arrangements among authorities and operators before travellers could use the new services. [^good-enough-ch01-1] A commuter choosing a journey and an authority deciding which journeys to make available face different problems. The commuter must get somewhere under today’s conditions. The authority can alter some of those conditions, but must work through budgets, contracts and political institutions. Chapter 15 returns to the work involved in providing those alternatives.

The traffic improvement also left questions about who benefited and who paid. People could accept that the charge reduced congestion while objecting to its cost or the use of its revenue. Support for continuation differed across the municipalities that held referendums. [^good-enough-ch01-2] Chapter 21 examines that public decision and the evidence available to inform it. Here, the buses arriving ahead of their schedules introduce a more immediate difficulty: a useful change in circumstances can leave people following instructions designed for the circumstances before it.

## When the sensible choice preserves the problem

Consider the traveller before any change in the rules. Leaving earlier might avoid some congestion, and another road might save time. Taking a bus might be preferable if its route and timetable fit the journey. Each adjustment concerns the alternatives available to that traveller and may be entirely reasonable. None gives that person the power to create a new bus service or determine the journeys of everyone else using the road.

The familiar instruction to make better choices is therefore incomplete when the difficulty lies in the choices on offer. Someone can recognise an attractive alternative and still lack the money, time, permission or cooperation needed to take it. Advice that ignores those requirements asks for an outcome without explaining how it could be achieved.

The same distinction applies in a workplace. In an invented case, a procedure is slow because staff have to enter the same information twice. An employee can become faster at the task, arrange the entries more carefully or reduce mistakes. The duplication survives, now performed to a higher standard. Improving the employee’s performance and changing the procedure are separate undertakings.

The traveller's position is what the Introduction called a *local minimum*: no change within the traveller's reach improves the journey, although another arrangement would serve everyone better. Before the trial, each driver's choice was a reasonable answer to the roads as they were. A driver who left the car at home would have made the road a little clearer for everyone else while gaining little personally, and a bus held up in the same traffic offered no faster journey. The better arrangement, with fewer cars and buses that could keep to their times, could not be reached by any one traveller's change. It needed many travellers to change together and someone with the authority to alter the conditions they all faced, which is what the new services and the charge did. Chapter 2 works the definition through in an example small enough to check by hand.

## The second entry

The invented office shows the idea at work where nobody is held up in traffic. Suppose the information goes once into the department's own system and again onto a form that another department keeps. Speed, care and accuracy, the improvements open to the employee, all leave the duplication where it is. The one change that would remove it, entering the information once, is not the employee's to make. Skipping the second entry would leave the other department without a record it expects, and whoever tried it would look careless. From where the employee stands, every step towards the better arrangement looks worse than doing the work twice and doing it well.

Several things can hold an arrangement like this in place, and one office can contain more than one of them. Nobody may know whether the second record is still read, and finding out takes time that belongs to no one's job. Merging the entries may need a programmer or a supplier, and so a budget, before it saves a single minute. It may be worth doing only if the other department changes its form as well. The two systems may have been set up and audited around each other, so that altering one means testing both again. The employee may also be assessed on the number of entries completed without error, a measure that rewards doing the duplication well and takes no account of whether it should be done at all. Each needs its own remedy. Chapters 5 to 9 take them one at a time.

The same inquiry can end with the duplication left in place. The second record might feed a return required by law, or be the copy an auditor relies on when the first system fails. A busy road, similarly, may carry traffic that people value, and a familiar process may provide a reliability that its replacement has yet to demonstrate. If the second entry turns out to be the price of something worth having, the arrangement is good enough. The useful result of the investigation is then to say so, and to stop treating the people who maintain it as the cause of a mistake. Before recommending change, we need to understand what an arrangement accomplishes as well as what it prevents.

The term has limits, and the book keeps them in view. In a human situation, what counts as an improvement and which changes are possible have to be established, and a diagram that places one outcome lower than another has already made that judgement. The idea helps most where it directs attention to particular constraints and possible changes. It helps least where the problem is an open conflict of interest: people who benefit from an arrangement may understand its costs to others perfectly well, and the relevant questions then concern power, rights, bargaining and the institutions through which a decision can be challenged.

In Stockholm, reduced traffic created the possibility of faster journeys, while the bus contracts still required the old arrival times. Someone then had to turn that possibility into an appropriate service. The difference between recognising an improvement and arranging for people to benefit from it is where much of this book’s work begins.
