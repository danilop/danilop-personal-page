During Stockholm’s congestion-charge trial, bus drivers reportedly had to slow down to avoid reaching their stops early. There was less traffic than their timetables allowed for. A British parliamentary delegation heard the account on a visit in July 2007, when it examined both the charging scheme and the contracts under which local buses operated. Those contracts required operators to follow an agreed schedule; a faster journey was not, by itself, permission to change it. [^good-enough-ch01-1]

The inconvenience was a consequence of an improvement. Passengers still needed buses to arrive when expected, including passengers who had not yet reached the stop. A timetable designed for congested streets could not make full use of clearer streets without being revised. The drivers’ response made sense within their instructions, even though the conditions that had helped determine those instructions had changed.

The congestion tax began on 3 January 2006, following an expansion of public transport. It applied to vehicles crossing a boundary around the inner city during charging hours, with higher charges at the busiest times. The charge was temporary: the trial would end in July, and residents would later vote on whether it should continue. Traffic crossing the boundary fell by roughly a fifth. [^good-enough-ch01-1][^good-enough-ch01-2]

This was a change in the circumstances of thousands of ordinary decisions. A journey that had previously involved fuel, parking and time now carried another cost. Travellers could respond in different ways. Some commuters switched to public transport, while other people made fewer trips or changed their destinations. The reduction did not consist entirely of former drivers occupying seats on buses and trains. When the charge was removed after the trial, traffic increased again, though it remained below its previous level. [^good-enough-ch01-2]

The transport system had produced one set of journeys under the previous rules and another under the trial. Neither result required everyone to agree about congestion charging, to make the same adjustment, or to become a different sort of person. A change in the available alternatives and their costs was enough to change many decisions.

The additional transport services required arrangements among authorities and operators before travellers could use them. [^good-enough-ch01-1] A commuter choosing a journey and an authority deciding which journeys to make available face different problems. The commuter must get somewhere under today’s conditions; the authority can alter some of those conditions, but must work through budgets, contracts and political institutions. Chapter 15 returns to the work involved in providing those alternatives.

The traffic improvement also left questions about who benefited and who paid. People could accept that the charge reduced congestion while objecting to its cost or the use of its revenue. Support for continuation differed across the municipalities that held referendums. [^good-enough-ch01-2] Chapter 21 examines that public decision and the evidence available to inform it. Here, the buses arriving ahead of their schedules introduce a more immediate difficulty: a useful change in circumstances can leave people following instructions designed for the circumstances before it.

## When the sensible choice preserves the problem

Consider the traveller before any change in the rules. Leaving earlier might avoid some congestion. Choosing another road might save time. Taking a bus might be preferable if its route and timetable fit the journey. Each adjustment concerns the alternatives available to that traveller, and each may be entirely reasonable. None gives that person the power to create a new bus service or determine the journeys of everyone else using the road.

A familiar instruction to make better choices can therefore be incomplete. The difficulty may concern the choices on offer. Someone can recognise an attractive alternative and still lack the money, time, permission or cooperation needed to take it. Advice that ignores those requirements asks for an outcome without explaining how it could be achieved.

The same distinction is useful in a workplace. A procedure may be slow because staff have to enter the same information twice. An employee can become faster at the task, arrange the entries more carefully or reduce mistakes. The duplication survives, now performed to a higher standard. Changing the procedure may require another department to accept a different record, a manager to approve the work and someone to check that an obligation has not been overlooked. Improving the employee’s performance and changing the procedure are separate undertakings. This hypothetical example introduces a distinction that will recur in the documented cases that follow.

It would be equally mistaken to conclude that every persistent arrangement is a mistake. A second entry might serve a necessary purpose. A road might be busy because many people value the destinations it connects. A familiar process may provide reliability that an alternative has not yet demonstrated. Before recommending change, we need to understand what the existing arrangement accomplishes as well as what it prevents.

The title borrows a term from optimisation. A *local minimum* is a solution whose objective value cannot be improved by sufficiently nearby feasible changes. It can be the best solution overall, but need not be. The difficulty examined here is the latter case: a rule for making local improvements can stop short of an alternative with a better result. The next chapter gives this definition a precise, accessible example.

In human situations, we also need to establish what counts as an improvement and which changes are possible. The costs of a transition and the right to refuse it require decisions of their own. A mathematical diagram becomes misleading if it answers these questions merely by placing one outcome lower than another. The judgement has already been made when the diagram is drawn.

Throughout the book, the term will be useful where it directs attention to particular constraints and possible changes. It will be less useful where the problem is an explicit conflict of interest. People who benefit from an arrangement may understand its costs to others perfectly well. In that situation, more explanation alone may achieve little; the relevant questions concern power, rights, bargaining and the institutions through which a decision can be challenged.

## Reading the book

This book is written for readers who make or influence decisions in a household, a workplace, a community or a public institution. It assumes ordinary experience of choices that involve trade-offs, and a willingness to examine an explanation even when it complicates a familiar story. It does not require prior study of mathematics, economics, physics or programming. Technical terms are explained when introduced, and optional appendices provide the formal detail needed to examine the models more closely.

The cases are central to the argument. A story about a change should show what people were trying to do, which alternatives they faced and what happened after they acted. Understanding the preparation and adjustment behind a successful outcome helps explain how it was achieved. When an attempt fails, we need to distinguish a poor proposal from an intervention that was never implemented as intended. The accounts include uncertainty and disagreement where the record requires them; those are part of the history rather than interruptions to it.

The first part examines why arrangements persist. It distinguishes incomplete information from an inability to afford change, and both from difficulties that require several people to act together. Earlier investments can also make an alternative harder to adopt, while an unsuitable measure of success can encourage work that defeats its stated purpose. These distinctions lead into cases in transport, healthcare, business and neighbourhood life, where more than one difficulty usually appears at once.

The second part considers what it takes to make a change possible. It moves from decisions within a household to the work of teams, communities, public authorities and international institutions. The scale matters because different people have different means of acting. A household can reconsider a purchase; it cannot supply its own national electricity system. A local organisation can gather evidence and negotiate a commitment; it may still need a public body to change a rule. Practical advice must identify who can perform the action it recommends.

The final part asks when change is justified and what should be preserved. Maintaining a satisfactory arrangement is a decision with its own demands. So is declining a proposal whose promised benefits depend on other people accepting serious risks. The book ends with a task that can be adapted to a decision you actually face: describe the problem with the people affected, establish what prevents improvement, and choose an action whose consequences can be assessed.

Numbered citations lead to notes grouped by chapter at the end. Those notes identify the sources for historical details, research findings and disputed accounts. The further-reading appendix offers a shorter route into the subjects for readers who want to continue. A memoir, an experiment, a mathematical result and an official evaluation supply different kinds of evidence; the distinction matters when drawing conclusions from them.

The practical aim is to leave you better able to describe a difficult decision and identify what would have to change for a different choice to become possible. The next step may be to gather evidence, or the evidence may already be adequate and the work concern financing, negotiation or authority. An alternative can also prove less attractive once its consequences are examined. A useful assessment must allow all of these results.

In Stockholm, reduced traffic created the possibility of faster journeys, while the bus contracts still required the old arrival times. Someone then had to turn that possibility into an appropriate service. The difference between recognising an improvement and arranging for people to benefit from it is where much of this book’s work begins.
